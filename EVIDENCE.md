# Relay's design choices, evidence-checked

Relay's core shape — individual phone calls, repeated over time, using a graduated cueing ladder, with a passive opt-in family dashboard — turns out to sit closer to the evidence-supported end of dementia-care research than a typical hackathon pitch would. The clearest wins: individual/structured/repeated retrieval beats one-off or group formats, closed/forced-choice questions beat open ones for getting a usable response, pull-based caregiver dashboards beat push alerts, and mornings beat evenings. The weakest link is the final "state the fact outright" reorientation rung — the two clinical traditions it sits between (errorless learning and validation/reality-orientation) don't actually support stating autobiographical facts as correction, and the honest fix is to narrow that rung's scope. Below, each concrete design choice is listed with the evidence behind it and, where relevant, a flagged gap. Citations are one line each, as requested — this is meant to be skimmed by judges in a few minutes, not read as a lit review.

## A. Which memories to surface, and when

- **Design individual, structured, repeated retrieval (not a single pass, not group format).** — Woods et al. 2018 Cochrane review (22 RCTs, n=1,972): individual reminiscence/life-review shows the clearest depression benefit (SMD −0.41 to −0.76 in newer meta-analyses), versus weak/inconsistent effects for unstructured group reminiscence. This is Relay's native format already — a phone call is inherently individual.
- **Lean into recurring calls over many sessions rather than a one-off "memory session."** — Woods et al. 2018 and a 2024/25 International Psychogeriatrics meta-analysis (24 RCTs, n=1,763): interventions with >8 sessions show significantly greater quality-of-life and behavioral-symptom benefit than shorter courses.
- **Resurface the same memory across multiple calls rather than treating each memory as a single retrieval event.** — El Haj et al. 2021 (Frontiers in Psychology, n=20 moderate-severe AD patients): repeated retrieval over just 6 sessions/2 weeks brought episodic recall quality up to matched-control levels, despite severe amnesia.
- **Open with "who"/relationship confirmation before asking "what happened."** — 2025 Frontiers in Neurology systematic review of autobiographical memory in AD: personal semantic memory (identity, relationships) stays relatively intact into moderate stages while episodic detail is impaired earliest — so identity/relationship questions are the safer, more answerable opening move, and this is also the direct neuropsychological justification for the ladder's ordering (free recall → context → association → recognition), not just intuition.
- **Weight older memories, especially roughly ages 6–30, higher by default.** — A Cortex study (25 AD patients vs. 30 matched controls) found a "reminiscence bump" for ages 6–30 followed by a steep drop for memories after 30 (Group × Life-period interaction p<.0001) — a disease-specific bump-then-cliff pattern, not just normal aging.
- **Also weight memories the person has told often (family lore, frequently-retold stories), not age alone.** — A companion study (14 AD patients) found retrieval-frequency, not chronological age, is the likely mechanism protecting a memory (Multiple Trace Theory) — if Relay's memory graph can capture "how often this was told," that's plausibly a better retrievability signal than age.
- **Don't categorically exclude very recent memories.** — One study in the same review found some patients recall very recent events (today/yesterday) better than intermediate-past ones — the retrievability curve may be U-shaped, not strictly "older is always better."
- **Gap, stated plainly:** no study tested a phone-delivered, AI/memory-graph-cued retrieval system like Relay — all reminiscence-therapy evidence is from human-facilitated, in-person sessions. Extrapolating to automated phone delivery is an inference the team should own, not hide. There's also no evidence breaking down the age/bump pattern by memory *category* (people vs. places vs. events), so Relay's category-prioritization logic is a design choice, not a directly evidenced one.

## B. Should the ladder end in "state the fact outright"?

**Recommendation: keep the reorientation rung, but narrow it — never use it to correct autobiographical/identity facts; reserve it for low-stakes procedural/functional information, and even then frame it as offering, not correcting.**

- **Errorless learning does not license stating an autobiographical fact ("Maya is your daughter") as correction.** — Baddeley & Wilson 1994 and the entire EL evidence base (including the largest RCT, REDALI-DEM, n=161) are about *new* procedural/associative learning (device use, ADL routines, face-name pairs) — never about retrieving a fact the person already knows but can't access. Citing EL to justify correcting identity content is an evidentiary overreach.
- **Even on EL's own home turf, the evidence is weaker than assumed.** — REDALI-DEM found errorless learning had no additional benefit over ordinary trial-and-error learning for new ADL tasks — so EL's strongest RCT undercuts the "errorless is proven superior" framing even before extending it further.
- **Validation therapy (the "never correct" tradition) is not strong proof either — its evidence base is nearly empty.** — Cochrane review (Neal & Barton Wright 2003): only 3 small RCTs, total n=116, "insufficient evidence to allow any conclusion about efficacy." Neither side of this debate can claim RCT-level backing.
- **Reality orientation — the real precedent for a "state the fact" rung — is contested and its supportive Cochrane review was withdrawn.** — Spector et al. 2000 Cochrane review found "some evidence of benefit" but was later withdrawn from the Cochrane Library; practitioner literature (ATrain Education CEU material) consistently documents that repeated correction in mid/late-stage dementia "only serve[s] to confuse and agitate."
- **The one place a stage-based reconciliation does appear in the literature: early-stage tolerates informational correction, mid/late-stage does not.** — ATrain Education practitioner-training material recommends reality orientation only in early-stage dementia, switching to validation-style acceptance once retention of corrected facts becomes impossible — moderate-strength practitioner consensus, not a peer-reviewed trial.
- **Kitwood's person-centered care framework provides theoretical (not RCT) backing for caution around correction.** — Kitwood's "malignant social psychology" vs. "positive person work" framing is the dominant conceptual lens in dementia care and cautions against interactions that undermine personhood, though the source found doesn't specifically discuss factual correction as an example.
- **Net design call:** allow the final rung for procedural/functional facts ("this button plays your message") — that's the domain EL evidence actually covers — but drop or heavily soften it for autobiographical/identity facts, and if retained at all for those, frame it as neutral information-offering rather than correction, and avoid anything resembling repeated insistent correction (the documented reality-orientation harm mechanism).
- **Gap, stated plainly:** no academic source directly names and resolves "errorless learning vs. validation therapy" as a conflict — the resolution above is the research team's own synthesis across adjacent literatures, not a single cited authority. No dementia-stage cutoff (e.g., "fine at GDS stage X, not Y") exists in a form precise enough to hard-code.

## C. How Relay should talk

- **Ask exactly one question per turn, closed/forced-choice rather than open-ended.** — Gerontologist 2024 systematic review of caregiver communication-training programs (citing the FOCUSED program, Ripich et al.): yes/no questions have the highest response rate of any question type; Alzheimer's Association and Alzheimer's San Diego guidance both converge on the same rule ("ask one question at a time," offer a binary choice instead of an open question).
- **Simplify by shortening sentences and cutting subordinate clauses, not by slowing speech rate.** — A controlled comprehension study (AD patients vs. healthy controls, varying speech rate and syntactic complexity) found speech *rate* did not significantly affect comprehension, but syntactic *complexity* did, with AD patients disproportionately hurt by complex sentences.
- **Don't treat a bare "yes" as ground truth — build in a confirmation or elaboration check.** — A 2026 SAGE study (n=267) found a "yes-no reversal" phenomenon (answering yes when meaning no, or vice versa) in 8.6% of dementia patients (up to ~18% in non-AD dementia, 0% in MCI); a separate Clinical Neuropsychologist 2012 study found yes/no and forced-choice recognition are not psychometrically interchangeable in MCI/AD. This is a genuine tension: yes/no questions get answered most reliably (per C's first point) but are also the format most vulnerable to false agreement — Relay should log response format alongside the answer rather than treat all "yes" answers as equally trustworthy.
- **Avoid the literal phrase "Do you remember...?"; use declarative/invitational framing instead ("I was just thinking about...", "Tell me about...").** — Practitioner/caregiver-advocacy commentary (not a controlled trial) argues this framing forces the person to either confess a memory failure or fabricate an answer; Alzheimer's Society BC's conversation-starter guidance is built entirely around this kind of declarative prompt instead.
- **Never correct or argue with a wrong/confabulated answer inside the call; route it out-of-band instead.** — Alzheimer's Association and Alzheimer's San Diego guidance converge: "don't correct or contradict," "respond to the feelings rather than the words" — consistent with section B's conclusion that in-call correction of autobiographical content is the higher-risk move.
- **Use a noticeably longer default silence timeout than a consumer voice assistant, and prefer a graduated response over one fixed threshold.** — A 2024 Frontiers in Dementia HCI paper found people with dementia produce longer and more frequent pauses/disfluencies than typical speakers, and that fixed-timeout voice assistants misfire by cutting people off; its recommended fix is "incremental clarification" (a gentle partial prompt after a first silence window, full hint only after a longer second window) rather than picking one number.
- **Gap, stated plainly: no source gives a validated numeric pause-duration threshold for dementia populations.** The only quantitative guidance found anywhere is the non-experimental practitioner rule of thumb, "allow plenty of time, then triple it." Relay's specific timeout values (e.g., "wait 4 seconds, then 8") are a design decision to pilot-test, not a number derived from research. Likewise, no controlled study directly measures distress caused by specific phrasing (e.g., "do you remember") — that guidance is consensus practitioner opinion, not RCT evidence.

## D. Does a caregiver dashboard help or harm?

**Recommendation: keep the dashboard exactly as designed — passive, opt-in, count-only, no severity scores or trend verdicts.** This configuration is the one every strand of adjacent evidence points toward, even though no single study tests this precise product.

- **Monitoring/eHealth tools for dementia caregivers show a small but real average benefit, with wide variance depending on design quality.** — A meta-analysis of 35 RCTs (n=3,388) found modest reductions in caregiver burden (SMD −0.26) and depressive symptoms (SMD −0.27), but a wide prediction interval meaning a poorly designed tool could land in the "no effect/harm" range — execution matters more than the mere existence of monitoring.
- **Push alerts, not monitoring itself, are the documented anxiety driver.** — A study of caregivers receiving remote activity-monitor alerts found ~38% reported negative reactions, driven specifically by false alarms and poorly timed (e.g., nighttime) notifications — this directly supports keeping Relay's dashboard pull-based rather than push/alert-driven.
- **People strongly prefer on-demand access to their own or a loved one's health information, and worry tracks the content, not the act of accessing it.** — A patient-portal study across 8,139 patients found 96% preferred immediate/on-demand access even to potentially concerning results, and only a small minority (4.9% for normal results) reported increased worry — supports opt-in "check when you want" design over default notifications.
- **Reassurance, not vigilance, is the primary benefit caregivers report from visibility into a relative's status.** — A qualitative study found 91–92% of caregivers viewed home-monitoring visibility positively, citing "peace of mind" as the main driver — this is the need Relay's dashboard should be designed to serve.
- **Keep the dashboard descriptive/behavioral ("recalled unaided in 6 of 8 calls"), never diagnostic or trend-verdict framing.** — Ambiguous loss theory (Pauline Boss, via Family Caregiver Alliance) holds that families cope best with bounded factual information that avoids forcing a verdict of "improving" or "declining"; separately, caregiver-ethics research on home surveillance found caregivers themselves want data scope and framing kept limited and contextualized, to avoid reducing their relative to a monitored/scored object.
- **Gap, stated plainly:** no study tests this exact product pattern — a passive, opt-in, count-only, non-clinical dashboard — head-to-head against anything. The recommendation above is a triangulation across ambiguous-loss theory, surveillance-ethics research, and alert-monitoring literature, not a single direct citation. There is also no dementia-specific study that isolates "decline dashboard"/severity-score framing as a tested harm — this appears to be a genuine literature gap, not a search failure.

## E. Call timing and session length

- **Default to morning calls; deprioritize late afternoon and evening.** — Wilks et al. 2021/2022 (n=169, high-frequency smartphone testing, the most methodologically robust study found): significantly worse associative-memory performance in evening vs. morning hours, more pronounced in participants with confirmed early AD pathology ("cognitive sundowning"). Smaller single-timepoint clinic studies (MMSE-based) didn't detect this effect, likely because MMSE is too coarse to catch it — not because the effect isn't real.
- **Treat late afternoon/evening as elevated-risk for agitation too, for a meaningful minority.** — Sundowning (late-day worsening of confusion/agitation) is estimated at ~20% of AD patients by the Alzheimer's Association, though prevalence estimates range 2.5%–66% across studies depending on definition — real, but not universal, so this is a "deprioritize," not a hard-block.
- **Cap calls at roughly 10 minutes, as a ceiling rather than a target.** — The closest structural analog found — an automated telephone reminiscence program for older adults (cognitively intact, not dementia-specific) — used calls "up to 10 minutes," 3x/week; staffed, multi-week therapy protocols (CST, digital 1:1 reminiscence) run 30–45 minutes but require a human facilitator managing pacing, which an automated call doesn't have.
- **Bias toward shorter (5–8 min) rather than the full 10, given the population.** — The 10-minute anchor comes from a cognitively-intact sample; dementia patients are generally more fatigue-prone, so treating 10 minutes as an upper bound rather than a default is the more conservative, defensible read of the analogy.
- **Gap, stated plainly:** no study measures within-session cognitive fatigue decline during conversation in a dementia population — the ~10-minute figure is an analogical anchor from a non-dementia phone-reminiscence trial, not a dementia-specific, directly-validated number. No study gives a precise "avoid after X pm" cutoff hour either; "late afternoon/evening" is as precise as the literature gets. No literature was found on optimal call *frequency* (e.g., is 3x/week too much) specifically for a memory-recall product.

## Open questions / evidence gaps

These are the genuine unresolved points the research surfaced — worth naming to judges as "we know this is unresolved," not glossed over:

- No study evaluates a system structurally like Relay (AI/phone-delivered, memory-graph-cued, cueing-ladder retrieval) — all reminiscence-therapy evidence is human-facilitated and in-person; Relay's whole approach is an extrapolation.
- No source directly names or resolves the "errorless learning vs. validation therapy" conflict as such — the stage-based and content-type-based resolution in this report is this team's own synthesis across adjacent literatures.
- No validated numeric pause/silence-duration threshold exists for dementia speech in conversational-agent design; Relay's specific timeout values must be pilot-tested, not derived from a cited number.
- No dementia-specific study measures within-call cognitive fatigue decline; the 10-minute call-length anchor comes from a non-dementia telephone-reminiscence trial.
- No study directly tests a "decline dashboard" or severity-score framing against a neutral/descriptive one in a family-facing dementia app — this appears to be a genuine gap in the literature, not a search miss.
- No controlled study measures distress/agitation as a function of specific phrasing (e.g., "do you remember" vs. alternatives); all phrasing guidance is practitioner consensus, not experimental evidence.
- Several foundational papers (Baddeley & Wilson 1994's full methods; Hodges & Graham 1990; the amnestic-MCI episodic-vs-semantic paper; the yes/no-vs-forced-choice recognition study's numeric results) were CAPTCHA-blocked or paywalled and could only be characterized via secondary citation, not verified against primary text.
- No study breaks down memory retrievability (the age-based "bump" or the retrieval-frequency effect) by category — people vs. places vs. events — which is exactly the axis Relay's memory-prioritization logic needs.
